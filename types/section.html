Book II Preface: Eudoxus & Archimedes in Stereometry

Book II, Prooemium (Schöne pp. 90–94; fols. 87r–88r). Having set forth in the First Book the mensuration of planar and curved surfaces, Hero opens the Second Book on solid bodies (στερεὰ σώματα) with a classic historical tribute: before Eudoxus of Cnidus, geometers did not possess rigorous proofs that every cylinder is triple the cone on the same base and of equal height ([XII.10]), nor that every prism is triple the pyramid; and after Eudoxus established those theorems by exhaustion, Archimedes surpassed all predecessors by proving that every sphere is two-thirds of its circumscribing cylinder (Sphere and Cylinder I.34).

Preface: Egyptian Origins & Orthogonal Unit Measures

Book I, Prooemium (Schöne pp. 2–6; fols. 67r–68r). Original geometry, just as the ancient account teaches us, was occupied with the measurements and distributions of land-parcels in Egypt—whence indeed it was called geometry (land-measurement). Necessity itself furnished human beings with the invention of the subject: for since the Nile inundated the plains of Egypt year by year, obliterating boundaries, sweeping away many plots, and augmenting others with alluvial deposits, it came to pass that the holdings of some proprietors were diminished while those of others were enlarged, so that each person could no longer recover an equal share without some exact method.
